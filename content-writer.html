Overview

What does a Content Writer do?

A Content Writer produces written material designed to inform, engage or educate a reader — blog posts, articles, website pages, product descriptions, email newsletters, and increasingly, content optimised to perform well in search engines (SEO content). The role sits at the intersection of writing, research and a basic understanding of how the content will actually be used — whether that's ranking on Google, explaining a product, or building a brand's voice.

Unlike engineering or medicine, there's no single licensing exam or mandatory degree — graduates from BJMC, BA English, BA Philosophy, Commerce or Science backgrounds all work as content writers. What consistently matters is the ability to write clearly, research accurately, and adapt tone for different audiences.

Fit check

Who should pursue content writing

This career is a good fit if you...

  • Genuinely enjoy writing regularly, not just occasionally for assignments.
  • Are self-motivated enough to build a portfolio on your own initiative, rather than waiting for a degree to hand you one.
  • Are comfortable researching unfamiliar topics quickly and writing about them accurately.

Think twice if you...

  • Want a career with a single clear qualifying exam or fixed entry path — this field rewards initiative more than credentials.
  • Dislike frequent feedback and editing on your work — revision is a constant part of professional writing.
  • Need predictable income immediately — freelance content writing especially can be inconsistent early on.
No fixed path

How people actually get in

  • No mandatory degree — any Class 12 stream and any bachelor's degree can lead here, since employers primarily assess writing samples.
  • A degree like BJMC or BA English can help build relevant skills and credibility, but is not strictly required.
  • A genuine portfolio — a personal blog, guest posts, college publication work, or freelance samples — matters more to most hiring managers than the degree name on your resume.

What actually gets you hired

Skills that matter most

Clear, error-free writingThe baseline requirement — grammar, structure and readability come before anything else.
Research abilityBeing able to research an unfamiliar topic quickly and represent it accurately, without plagiarising.
Basic SEO understandingKnowing how to structure content for search visibility — keywords, headings, meta descriptions — is now a near-standard expectation.
Tone adaptabilityWriting differently for a formal B2B audience versus a casual social media post is a skill recruiters actively test for.
Editing disciplineBeing able to revise your own work critically, and take feedback well, rather than treating a first draft as final.
Basic tool familiarityComfort with CMS platforms (like WordPress), Google Docs collaboration, and increasingly, AI writing tools used responsibly as aids, not replacements.

Growing without a fixed degree ladder

Upskilling & specialisation

Rather than a fixed postgraduate ladder, most content writers grow by specialising — into technical writing, UX writing, SEO strategy, or a specific industry niche like finance or healthcare content, where subject expertise commands higher pay. Short certifications in SEO, content marketing or copywriting (from platforms like HubSpot Academy or Google) can help, and some writers eventually move into content strategy, editorial leadership, or an MBA in Marketing if they want a more managerial track.

Honest take

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages

  • Low barrier to entry — you can start building a portfolio while still in college, without waiting for a specific degree.
  • Genuine flexibility — full-time, freelance, or a hybrid mix are all realistic ways to work.
  • Growing demand as more businesses invest in content marketing and SEO.

Disadvantages

  • No fixed credential means competition is broad and entry-level pay can be modest until you specialise.
  • Freelance income can be unpredictable, especially before you build a stable client base.
  • The rise of AI writing tools is changing the field, pushing writers toward higher-value strategy and editing work over basic content production.
A commonly confused distinction

Content Writer vs Copywriter

FactorContent WriterCopywriter
Primary goalInform, engage or educate the readerPersuade the reader to take a specific action
Typical outputBlog posts, articles, guides, long-form contentAd copy, taglines, landing pages, email subject lines
Typical lengthLonger-form, often 800+ wordsShort, punchy, every word counted
Core skillResearch, structure & clarityPersuasion, brand voice & concision

The two roles overlap considerably in practice, and many writers do both — but understanding the distinction helps you tailor your portfolio and pitch to the right kind of role.

Common myths

Myths vs facts

Myth: Anyone who can write in English can be a professional content writer.

Fact: Professional content writing needs structured research, SEO awareness, tone control and consistent quality under deadlines — genuine, developed skills beyond conversational fluency.

Myth: You need a BJMC or English degree specifically to be taken seriously.

Fact: Most hiring managers care far more about your writing samples than your degree — strong portfolios from unrelated backgrounds are hired regularly.

Myth: AI writing tools have made content writers obsolete.

Fact: AI tools have changed workflows, but skilled writers who can research accurately, edit critically and add genuine expertise or voice remain in demand — the role is shifting, not disappearing.
