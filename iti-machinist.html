Overview

What is the Machinist trade?

  • ITI Machinist is a 2-year, NCVT/DGT-affiliated trade under the Craftsman Training Scheme, training students to operate lathes, milling machines, grinding machines and shapers to manufacture precision metal parts.
  • Unlike Fitter, which focuses on manual assembly, Machinist centres specifically on machine-tool operation and precision measurement.
  • Training is heavily hands-on — reading engineering drawings, setting up machines, and producing parts to tight tolerances — combined with trade theory and workshop calculation, following the standard Craftsmen Training Scheme (CTS) structure.

  • Many institutes now also introduce basic Computer Numerical Control (CNC) machine exposure as part of the modernised syllabus.
Fit check

Who should choose this trade

✔ Ideal for you if…

  • Enjoy precise, exacting work and are comfortable operating machinery.
  • Want a skilled trade with strong demand in precision manufacturing and machine shops.
  • Are interested in eventually specialising in modern CNC machine operation.

✖ Reconsider if…

  • Prefer broader assembly/maintenance work over specialised machine operation — Fitter fits better.
  • Are uncomfortable with repetitive, precision-focused tasks requiring sustained concentration.
  • Want to work specifically with lathes alone rather than multiple machine types — Turner may suit you better.

Beyond this trade

Higher studies & progression

Many institutes allow lateral entry into Diploma in Mechanical/Production Engineering for ITI Machinist holders, typically into a later year, though rules vary by state and institute. Dedicated CNC operator/programmer certifications are widely pursued afterward and can significantly boost pay and job options, since CNC skills are increasingly valued across manufacturing. Railways and defence technical recruitment exams are also common direct next steps.

Honest take

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages

  • Genuinely skilled, in-demand trade at the core of precision manufacturing.
  • Clear specialisation path toward higher-paying CNC operator roles.
  • Strong government/PSU and defence technical recruitment access.

Disadvantages

  • CNC exposure quality varies significantly by institute and available equipment.
  • Repetitive, precision-focused work that requires sustained concentration.
  • Narrower than Fitter in terms of general assembly/maintenance job scope.

Good to know

Frequently asked questions

Machinist is a broader trade covering multiple machine tools — lathes, milling machines, grinders and shapers — for a range of precision manufacturing tasks. Turner is a narrower, lathe-specific trade focused specifically on turning operations. Machinist offers wider machine-shop versatility; Turner offers deeper specialisation in one machine type.
Machinist is typically a 2-year NCVT-affiliated trade, combining trade theory, engineering drawing, workshop calculation and substantial hands-on machine operation practice.
Many institutes now include CNC basics as part of the modernised syllabus, though depth of CNC exposure varies significantly by institute and available equipment. Dedicated CNC operator certifications are also commonly pursued afterward to strengthen employability.
Precision manufacturing, automobile component makers, tool and die companies, heavy engineering firms, and machine shops across manufacturing hubs all hire trained machinists, alongside government/PSU and defence technical recruitment routes.
Machinist trade-qualified candidates can apply for various Railways (Railway Recruitment Board (RRB) Technician), defence technical trades, and Public Sector Undertaking (PSU) manufacturing/maintenance posts, each through its own separate recruitment exam.
