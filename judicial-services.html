Judicial Services — Becoming a Judge in India

9 min read · State-level government exam, conducted separately by each state · Requires an LLB degree

Judicial Services lets a law graduate become a Civil Judge through a dedicated state-level competitive exam — one of the more stable, respected career routes available after an LLB, and unlike some assumptions, doesn't require years of prior litigation practice in most states. This guide covers eligibility, the exam pattern, and how it compares to building a litigation practice instead.

Overview

What are Judicial Services?

Judicial Services refers to the state-level recruitment process through which law graduates are appointed as Civil Judges (Junior Division), the entry point into India's subordinate judiciary. Each state conducts its own exam — commonly called the PCS-J (Provincial Civil Services - Judicial) or Judicial Services Examination — through its Public Service Commission or under High Court supervision, since there's no single all-India judiciary exam like UPSC.

Once appointed, a Civil Judge presides over cases within their jurisdiction and progresses through a structured career ladder within the subordinate judiciary, eventually becoming eligible for elevation to more senior judicial positions with experience.

Fit check

Who should pursue Judicial Services

This path is a good fit if you...

  • Want a stable, respected government career with a clear structure, sooner than independent litigation typically offers.
  • Are prepared to commit serious, sustained study to a genuinely difficult, low-vacancy competitive exam.
  • Value the neutrality, discipline and responsibility that comes with sitting in judgment on others' disputes.

Think twice if you...

  • Want maximum earning potential quickly — corporate law firms generally pay more than early judicial service salaries.
  • Prefer flexibility and independence over a structured government service with transfer postings.
  • Are not willing to prepare seriously — vacancy numbers each year are small relative to applicants in most states.
Eligibility

Who can apply for Judicial Services

  • An LLB degree (3-year or 5-year integrated) from a recognised university, and enrolment as an advocate with a State Bar Council in most states.
  • Age limits typically range from 21-35 years for Civil Judge (Junior Division), varying by state, with relaxations for reserved categories.
  • Most states allow fresh LLB graduates to apply directly, without a minimum years-of-practice requirement, though a few states set specific conditions — always check your target state's current notification.

Three-stage process

Exam pattern

Preliminary examObjective-type paper covering law subjects (Civil/Criminal Procedure, Evidence, Constitutional Law) and general knowledge — a screening round to shortlist candidates for Mains.
Mains examWritten, descriptive papers covering substantive and procedural law, language papers (often including the state's regional language) and an essay/general English paper.
Viva-voce / InterviewFinal stage assessing legal knowledge, judgment and suitability for judicial office, for candidates who clear the Mains.
State variationExact papers, weightage and language requirements differ by state — always refer to your target state's official notification for the current pattern.

Where it can lead

Career progression

Civil Judge (Junior Division)Entry-level judicial post, handling civil and criminal matters within defined pecuniary/jurisdictional limits.
Civil Judge (Senior Division)Promotion after a set number of years of satisfactory service, with a wider range of case jurisdiction.
District JudgeSenior position overseeing a district's judiciary, reached through further seniority-based promotion or a separate direct-recruitment exam.
High Court / Supreme CourtNot reached through this exam directly — appointed separately through a collegium-based process, usually via elevation from the subordinate judiciary or from senior advocates.
Honest take

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages

  • Stable government salary and job security, without needing to build a client base first.
  • Highly respected role in society, with a clearly defined promotion structure.
  • Fresh graduates can attempt it directly in most states, without years of prior practice.

Disadvantages

  • Very few vacancies each year relative to the number of law graduates who apply in most states.
  • Different exam pattern, syllabus and language requirement in every state, so preparation isn't easily transferable.
  • Postings and transfers within the state are part of government service, offering less location flexibility than private practice.

Common myths

Myths vs facts

Myth: You need years of litigation experience before you can become a judge.

Fact: In most states, fresh LLB graduates can directly appear for the Civil Judge (Junior Division) exam — this differs from higher judiciary appointments, which do typically require significant experience.

Myth: There's a single national judiciary exam like UPSC.

Fact: Each state conducts its own Judicial Services exam separately, with different eligibility, syllabus and vacancy numbers — there is no unified all-India judiciary exam currently in place.

Myth: Passing this exam makes you a High Court judge.

Fact: This exam only leads to entry-level Civil Judge positions in the subordinate judiciary — High Court and Supreme Court judges are appointed separately through a collegium-based process.
